Rutherford's interpretation of α-particle scattering by gold foil

Every α-particle path below is obtained by numerically integrating Newton's equations under the Coulomb force of a lattice of gold nuclei — nothing is drawn by hand. Drag the orange particle to change how close it passes to a nucleus, and use the nuclear-size slider to squeeze the atom's positive charge into a point (Rutherford) or smear it over the whole atom (Thomson).

This particle met a second nucleus on its way out, so its final direction is not the single-scattering value. Rutherford's analysis needs a foil thin enough that this is rare — here the foil is drawn only four atoms thick but the charge is exaggerated, so it happens occasionally.
The slider is non-linear: it resolves the last ten-thousandth of an atomic radius, because that is the only place a large deflection lives — which is exactly why large deflections are rare. For a point nucleus the first readout obeys tan(θ/2) = b0/b exactly; the drawn curve is the full numerical orbit through all four layers.

2 · Size of the positive charge

point nucleus (Rutherford)whole atom (Thomson)
The charge Ze is held fixed and spread uniformly through a sphere of radius R. Outside it the force is the exact Coulomb K/r2; inside it the enclosed-charge law gives Kr/R3. Making R large caps the maximum force an α particle can ever feel — which is precisely why the plum-pudding atom cannot turn one around.

Computed live by firing 241 α particles, uniformly spaced across one full atomic cell, through the four-layer lattice. Percentages are for the exaggerated geometry drawn here. In the real Geiger–Marsden foil about 1 in 8000 α particles came back — still impossible for a plum-pudding atom, which is the whole argument.

Scales. As in every textbook version of this figure, the nucleus is drawn enormously too large: a real gold nucleus has about 1/20 000 of the atom's radius, so at this atom size it would be a fifth of a pixel across. For the same reason the Coulomb strength is scaled up about a hundredfold, to b0 = K/(mv2) = 0.01 a, where a is the atomic radius — with the true value (≈10−4 a) no bend would be visible at this magnification. Everything else — the orbit shapes, the angles, the ratios between them, and the way they respond to b and R — is exact. Dots smaller than 2.6 px are drawn at 2.6 px so they stay visible; the physics always uses the true R.
