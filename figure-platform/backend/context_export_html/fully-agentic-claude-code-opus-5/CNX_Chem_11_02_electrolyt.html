Nonelectrolytes, strong electrolytes, and weak electrolytes

Three identical conductivity cells: two electrodes in solution, wired in series with a light bulb and a 120 V source. The bulb is the meter — it can only glow if dissolved ions carry current between the plates. Change the solute concentration and watch both the particle picture and the bulb respond.

What the three cells show

ethanol dissolves as whole, neutral C2H5OH molecules. No charge carriers, no current, dark bulb — a nonelectrolyte. KCl dissociates completely (α = 1), so every formula unit supplies two mobile ions — a strong electrolyte. Acetic acid reaches an equilibrium in which only a small percentage of molecules are ionised — a weak electrolyte, so the same total concentration gives far fewer carriers and a dim bulb.

The numbers are really computed

Weak-acid ionisation from the exact quadratic root of Ka = α²c/(1−α) with Ka(CH3COOH)=1.8×10−5; strong-electrolyte molar conductivity from Kohlrausch Λ = Λ° − A√c. Then κ = Λαc, cell resistance R = (d/A)/κ with d = 4 cm and A = 25 cm², and a series circuit with a 120 V / 60 W bulb (240 Ω) gives the current, the bulb power and — via the incandescent law L ∝ (V/V°)3.5 — the glow you see.

Try this

Drag the concentration slider down to 0.001 mol/L. The KCl bulb fades because there are simply fewer ions, yet the printed α for acetic acid rises from ~1.3% to ~12% — Ostwald's dilution law: a weak electrolyte ionises more completely as it is diluted, even though its total conductivity still falls. Ethanol stays dark at every concentration: dissolving more of a nonelectrolyte never creates a carrier.

Particle pictures are schematic: the drawn number of particles and the drawn ion fraction for acetic acid are exaggerated so that single ions remain visible. All printed quantities (α, ion molarity, κ, R, I, bulb power) are calculated from the constants above.
