Nuclear fission of uranium‑235

A slow (thermal) neutron is absorbed by a 235U nucleus, forming the excited compound nucleus 236U. It deforms, pinches at the middle and splits into two unequal daughter nuclei — 141Ba and 92Kr — ejecting three neutrons and a large burst of energy. Every sphere below is a real nucleon: the clusters are packed by an actual 3‑D sphere‑packing computation, so each nucleus has exactly the right number of protons (green) and neutrons (grey), and the deformed 236U has the same volume as the sphere it came from.

Mass number and atomic number are conserved at every step, but mass is not: the products are about 0.1 % lighter than the reactants, and that missing mass appears as roughly 200 MeV of kinetic energy and radiation per fission (≈ 2×1010 kJ per mole of 235U, about ten million times the energy per mole from burning coal). 141Ba and 92Kr are only two of many possible fragment pairs; the split is asymmetric and statistical. Because each event consumes one neutron and emits about three, the reaction can sustain itself once the sample is large enough that fewer than two of those three escape — the critical mass.
