Polar molecules in an electric field

Each HF molecule is a permanent dipole: the electronegative fluorine carries δ−, the hydrogen δ+. With no field the molecules tumble into random orientations (a). Switch the field on and the torque τ = μE sin φ twists every dipole until δ+ faces the negative plate (b) — but only as far as thermal jostling allows.

Thermal jostling integrates the rotational Langevin equation dφ = −(μE/kT) sinφ dt + √(2dt) ξ, whose steady state is exactly the Boltzmann distribution above. Turn it on to see partial alignment as a time average rather than a frozen snapshot.

Alignment vs. field strength

Reality check. A laboratory field of 107 V m−1 (already near the breakdown strength of air) gives μE/kT ≈ 0.015 and ⟨cos φ⟩ ≈ 0.007 — a very slight net alignment on top of near–random tumbling. The textbook drawing exaggerates the effect so the direction of the alignment is visible; the slider lets you reach that idealized picture.
