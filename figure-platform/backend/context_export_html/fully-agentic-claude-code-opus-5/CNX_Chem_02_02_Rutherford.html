The Geiger–Rutherford gold foil experiment

A collimated beam of α particles from a radium source strikes a thin gold foil at the centre of a circular luminescent screen. The screen wraps all the way around the foil, so a scattered α can be detected at any angle — including straight back toward the source. Fire the beam and watch where the particles land.

Deflection angle distribution

Counts on a logarithmic scale. The steep fall-off is Rutherford's 1/sin⁴(θ/2) law — it is an output of the simulated collisions, not something drawn in.

How the model computes each collision. Every atomic layer the α crosses presents one gold nucleus at a random impact parameter b, sampled uniformly by area (P(<b) = nπb²). Rutherford's exact relation tan(θ/2) = d/2b, with collision diameter d = 2·2ZAue²/4πε₀E = 29.5 fm for a 7.7 MeV α on gold, fixes the deflection at that layer; the deflections then add along the path, and an α turned past 90° leaves the foil. Nothing about the angular distribution is hard-coded.

Why the thickness slider matters. Adding layers makes small deflections pile up — the transmitted beam spreads out roughly as √(layers). Large-angle events grow only in direct proportion to thickness and stay rare: no stack of small deflections can turn an α around, so a backscattered α must come from one violent close encounter.

Why it killed the plum-pudding atom. If positive charge were smeared through the whole 10⁻¹⁰ m atom, the largest force an α could feel would be thousands of times too weak to reverse it. Rutherford measured roughly 1 α in 8 000 coming back — "as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." All of the positive charge and nearly all the mass had to be packed into a nucleus ~10⁻¹⁴ m across.
