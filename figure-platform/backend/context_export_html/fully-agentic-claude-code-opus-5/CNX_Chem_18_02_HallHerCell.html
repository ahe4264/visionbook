The Hall–Héroult cell: electrolytic production of aluminium

Al2O3 is dissolved in molten cryolite, Na3AlF6, which melts near 1000 °C instead of the 2072 °C needed for pure alumina. Al3+ is reduced to molten aluminium on the carbon cathode floor; O2− is oxidised at the carbon anodes, which are burned away as O2, CO and CO2.

Controls

40 kA

Raising the current speeds up ion transport, multiplies the gas bubbles on the anodes and makes the molten-aluminium pad thicken faster (Faraday's law). Simulated time runs at 1 s ≈ 2 h, because a real metal pad grows only a few centimetres per day.

Al3+ → cathode O2− → anode e− in the circuit CO2 CO O2

What to watch

  • Blue Al3+ ions sink to the sloping carbon floor, pick up 3 e− each and join the bright pool of molten aluminium — the pad thickens as charge passes.
  • Red O2− ions migrate to the carbon anodes; every arrival nucleates a gas bubble that slides out from under the anode and rises up its side to the crust, where it leaves with the HF and particulates.
  • Electrons are pulled out of the anodes, up the rods, along the (+) bus bar, and are returned by the rectifier through the (−) bar into the cathode.
  • As the pad grows the anode–metal gap shrinks, so the IR drop and the cell voltage fall — until the cell would short. That is why the metal is tapped roughly once a day.
